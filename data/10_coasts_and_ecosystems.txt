Coastal Ecosystems: Mangroves, Estuaries and Seagrass

Coastal ecosystems form the link between land and sea and are some of the most productive places on Earth.

Mangroves are salt tolerant trees that grow in tropical and subtropical tidal areas. Their tangled roots stabilise the shoreline, trap sediment, filter water and act as nurseries for young fish, crabs and shrimp. Mangroves also reduce the impact of storms and tsunamis. The Sundarbans, shared by India and Bangladesh, is the largest mangrove forest in the world and home to the Bengal tiger. In Tamil Nadu, the Pichavaram mangrove forest near Chidambaram is one of the largest in India.

An estuary is a partly enclosed coastal area where fresh water from rivers mixes with salty seawater. Estuaries are rich in nutrients and support fisheries, migratory birds and wetlands. Lagoons such as Chilika Lake in Odisha, Asia's largest brackish water lagoon, are related coastal water bodies.

Seagrass meadows are underwater flowering plants that grow in shallow sandy water. They provide food and shelter for dugongs, sea turtles and fish, and they store large amounts of carbon. The Gulf of Mannar and Palk Bay region in southern India contains important seagrass beds and is a habitat for dugongs.

Kelp forests are underwater forests of large brown algae found in cool, nutrient rich coastal waters. Salt marshes and sandy beaches are other coastal habitats. Coastal erosion, urban development, aquaculture ponds and sea level rise threaten these ecosystems.
