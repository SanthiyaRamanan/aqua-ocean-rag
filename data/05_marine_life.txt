Marine Life and Food Webs

The ocean is home to life ranging from microscopic plankton to the blue whale, the largest animal ever known to exist, which can grow to about 30 metres long. Scientists estimate that a majority of marine species have not yet been described.

Plankton are organisms that drift with the currents. Phytoplankton are plant-like and make their own food through photosynthesis, forming the base of most marine food webs. Zooplankton are small animals that eat phytoplankton and are in turn eaten by fish, jellyfish and whales.

A marine food chain typically runs from phytoplankton to zooplankton to small fish such as sardines, then to larger fish such as tuna, and finally to top predators such as sharks, orcas and humans. Energy is lost at each step, so there are far fewer top predators than prey.

Marine mammals such as whales, dolphins and seals breathe air and are warm-blooded. Whales are divided into baleen whales, which filter tiny food through baleen plates, and toothed whales, such as dolphins and sperm whales. Sea turtles, marine iguanas and sea snakes are examples of marine reptiles.

Sharks are fish with skeletons made of cartilage rather than bone. They have existed for more than 400 million years. Many shark species are important apex predators that keep fish populations healthy. Octopuses are intelligent invertebrates with three hearts and blue blood.

Many deep sea animals produce their own light through bioluminescence, used to attract prey, find mates or confuse predators. Hydrothermal vents on the seafloor support entire communities that depend on chemosynthesis, where bacteria make energy from chemicals such as hydrogen sulfide instead of sunlight.
