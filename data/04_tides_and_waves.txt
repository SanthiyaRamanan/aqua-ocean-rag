Tides, Waves and Tsunamis

Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level caused mainly by the gravitational pull of the Moon, and to a lesser extent the Sun. Most coasts have two high tides and two low tides each day. The time between two high tides is about 12 hours and 25 minutes.

Spring tides occur at new moon and full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up, producing the highest high tides and lowest low tides. Neap tides occur at the quarter moons, when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles, and the tidal range is smallest. The names have nothing to do with the season.

The Bay of Fundy in Canada has the largest tidal range in the world, which can exceed 15 metres.

Most ocean waves are created by wind blowing over the water surface. The size of a wave depends on wind speed, how long the wind blows and the distance it blows over, known as the fetch. The highest point of a wave is the crest, the lowest is the trough, and the distance between two crests is the wavelength.

A tsunami is a series of very long waves usually triggered by an undersea earthquake, landslide or volcanic eruption. In deep water a tsunami may be barely noticeable, but it slows and grows taller as it approaches shallow coastal water. Tsunamis are not caused by wind or tides. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, triggered by a magnitude 9 earthquake off Sumatra, affected coastlines including India, Sri Lanka and Indonesia.

A rip current is a narrow, strong flow of water moving away from the shore. Swimmers caught in one should swim parallel to the beach rather than directly against the current.
