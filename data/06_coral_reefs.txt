Coral Reefs

Coral reefs are underwater structures built by colonies of tiny animals called coral polyps. Polyps secrete calcium carbonate skeletons, and over thousands of years these accumulate into reefs. Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor but support about 25 percent of all marine species, which is why they are called the rainforests of the sea.

Most reef-building corals live in a partnership with microscopic algae called zooxanthellae that live inside their tissues. The algae give corals their colour and provide most of their food through photosynthesis. In return the coral gives the algae shelter and nutrients.

Coral bleaching happens when corals are stressed, most often by unusually warm water, and expel their algae. The coral turns white and, if the stress continues, can starve and die. Other threats include ocean acidification, pollution, overfishing, destructive fishing methods and coastal development.

Reef-building corals prefer clear, shallow, warm water, typically between about 23 and 29 degrees Celsius, in tropical regions. The Great Barrier Reef off Queensland, Australia is the largest coral reef system in the world and can be seen from space. In India, coral reefs are found in the Gulf of Mannar, the Gulf of Kutch, the Andaman and Nicobar Islands and Lakshadweep.

Reefs protect coastlines by absorbing wave energy, provide food and jobs for hundreds of millions of people, and support tourism and fishing. Types of reef include fringing reefs, barrier reefs and atolls.

Mangroves and seagrass beds often grow near reefs and work together with them as connected coastal ecosystems.
