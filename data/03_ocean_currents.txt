Ocean Currents and Circulation

Ocean currents are continuous movements of seawater. Surface currents are mainly driven by wind, the Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect) and the shape of the continents. They form huge circular systems called gyres, which turn clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and anticlockwise in the Southern Hemisphere.

The Gulf Stream is a warm, fast current that carries water from the Gulf of Mexico across the North Atlantic toward Europe, helping keep western Europe milder than other places at the same latitude. The Kuroshio Current plays a similar role in the Pacific near Japan.

Deep currents are driven by differences in water temperature and salinity, which together determine density. This is called thermohaline circulation. Cold, salty water near Greenland and Antarctica becomes dense and sinks, then flows slowly along the deep ocean floor. This system is often called the global conveyor belt, and one full loop can take around 1,000 years.

Upwelling happens when winds push surface water away from a coast and cold, nutrient-rich water rises from below. Upwelling zones such as those off Peru and Namibia support some of the richest fisheries in the world.

El Nino is a periodic warming of the surface of the central and eastern Pacific that changes weather patterns around the world. La Nina is the opposite cool phase. Together they are called the El Nino Southern Oscillation, or ENSO.

In the northern Indian Ocean, currents reverse direction with the seasons because of the monsoon winds, which is unusual compared with other oceans.
