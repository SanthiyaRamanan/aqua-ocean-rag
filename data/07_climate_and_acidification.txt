Climate Change and Ocean Acidification

The ocean has absorbed more than 90 percent of the extra heat trapped by greenhouse gases and roughly a quarter to a third of the carbon dioxide released by human activity. This has slowed atmospheric warming but is changing the ocean itself.

Ocean acidification is the ongoing decrease in the pH of seawater caused by absorbing carbon dioxide. When CO2 dissolves in seawater it forms carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and lowers pH. Since the start of the industrial era, the average surface ocean pH has dropped from about 8.2 to about 8.1, which represents roughly a 30 percent increase in acidity because the pH scale is logarithmic.

Acidification makes it harder for shell-building animals such as corals, oysters, clams, sea urchins and some plankton to form their calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. This can disrupt food webs.

Sea level is rising for two main reasons: the melting of land ice such as glaciers and ice sheets, and thermal expansion, which means warmer water takes up more volume. Low lying coasts and island nations are especially vulnerable.

Marine heatwaves, periods of unusually high sea temperatures, are becoming more frequent and cause coral bleaching, fish kills and loss of kelp forests. Warming also reduces the oxygen dissolved in water, creating low oxygen dead zones.

Blue carbon refers to carbon stored by coastal ecosystems such as mangroves, salt marshes and seagrass meadows. These habitats store carbon very efficiently, so protecting them helps fight climate change.
